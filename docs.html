California Wolf Data Explorer work in progress

An interactive map and AI assistant for exploring the return of gray wolves to California — pack territories and movement alongside the habitat, connectivity, conservation, and land-use data that shapes where wolves can live.

About this project

A project of the Eric and Wendy Schmidt Center for Data Science & Environment (DSE) at UC Berkeley, developed with the California Wolf Project at UC Berkeley.

Gray wolves (Canis lupus) were extirpated from California by the 1920s. In 2011 a single wolf, OR-7, crossed back into the state; today California is home to a growing number of established packs across the northern Sierra Nevada and southern Cascades. Their recolonization raises pressing questions for wildlife managers, ranchers, tribes, and the public — where will wolves go next, what habitat and prey support them, and where will they overlap with people and livestock?

This app brings the California Department of Fish and Wildlife (CDFW) gray wolf monitoring data together with the environmental, habitat-connectivity, conservation, and socioeconomic context needed to interpret it — and pairs it with an AI assistant that can drive the map and answer analytical questions about the underlying data. It is a work in progress; layers and capabilities are being added and refined.

Reading the wolf movement data

CDFW does not publish GPS points, and this map cannot show you where a wolf went. It shows which cell a collar was reported in, and when.

⬡ A dot is a cell, not a location. Each cell CDFW publishes is the hexagon, on a fixed grid, that contains one collar's most recent reported location. CDFW picked the cell size from how far wolves typically move in a day and to keep it “large enough to protect exact wolf locations”; it publishes no figure for the size. Measured from the cells themselves, they are about 37 km² — 14 square miles, roughly 7 km (4 miles) flat-to-flat and 8 km (5 miles) corner to corner. That cell is the finest location that exists in this data, and it marks one fix rather than a day's wandering. Where the map draws a dot or a label, it sits at the centre of that cell: a marker for the hexagon, not a position within it.

There is no known path between cells. Positions arrive as a series of snapshots, roughly every two hours, each naming the cell a collar reported from. What happened between one cell and the next was never observed. Two consecutive cells 25 km apart tell you the wolf covered at least that distance somehow — not the route, and not the speed.

Two ways to play it back

The Cell-to-Cell Movement layer has a mode switch in its playback controls, because the two views make quite different claims:

  • Observed only (the default) — each cell is drawn as a hexagon, for exactly the window it was being reported, and then the hexagon jumps to wherever the wolf was reported next. Nothing is drawn during a break in reporting. What you see is what was recorded.
  • Interpolated — a dot glides steadily from one cell centre to the next. It is much easier to follow, and none of it is data: the position between two reports is invented by the animation, at a constant speed along a straight line nobody observed. Use it to read the sequence, not the movement.

Neither mode draws a trail line behind the animation. A line through consecutive cell centroids looks like a recorded track, and no such track exists.

What the data cannot tell you

  • Not all wolves are collared. CDFW aims for at least one collar per pack and prioritizes packs with higher known or potential livestock conflict — so this is a biased subset, not a sample. Wolves and packs cannot be counted from it. Wolves in a pack also move independently, so one collar's cell does not locate the rest of the pack: CDFW's own warning is that “wolf-livestock conflict can and will take place beyond the boundaries of active cells.”
  • An empty area is not an empty area. Collars take around four fixes a day and transmit each morning, transmission is delayed or lost to weather and forest canopy, and from roughly April through August cells near den and rendezvous sites are not activated, to protect them. No cell in a territory does not mean no wolves.
  • A stationary hexagon may be a silent collar. If a collar has not reported for a day, the map keeps showing its last known cell. The last-fix date on each cell (most_recent_adj) is what tells you whether a cell is current.
  • Each identifier is one wolf, not a pack. Labels such as CA091 are individual collared animals. CDFW changed its naming convention on 1 July 2026: wolves now get a sequential CA### id in the order they were identified, replacing the old pack-prefixed form (WHA23F was a Whaleback female). The two are not convertible by rule — CDFW publishes the crosswalk in California's Known Wolves — which is why some labels here still read “CA127 (previously Whaleback 2)”.
  • A wolf's pack is where it came from, not where it is. The pack field on a cell is the wolf's origin pack — the one it was born into. The animals this map is most used to watch are exactly the ones that have left: CA124 was born in Whaleback and is now in the Ashpan pack, CA077 in Whaleback and is now the Whitehorse female, CA091 in Beyem Seyo and ranges in Yowlumni country. Don't read the pack label as a current address, or as a link to the territory polygon of the same name.
  • The pack list here is a February 2025 snapshot. Those six territories were the packs CDFW recognized then. Its July 2026 list is longer — Ashpan, Tunnison, Grizzly, Interstate, Whitehorse and Long Valley have since been recognized, several promoted from Areas of Wolf Activity; Diamond and Beyem Seyo are now separate entries; and Beyem Seyo is a past pack, its remaining adults lethally removed in October 2025 after repeated livestock depredations in Sierra Valley. A pack missing from this map is not a pack that doesn't exist.
  • Territory polygons are approximate, and that estimate is ours. The six pack polygons were hand-digitized here from CDFW's February 2025 Approximate Area of Gray Wolf Activity map, and we put their accuracy at roughly 5–10 miles (8–16 km) — our georeferencing estimate, not a CDFW figure. CDFW describes its own map as showing “the approximate boundaries of known resident wolf territories”. They are areas of activity, not boundaries or home ranges, they do not update, and later editions of the CDFW map supersede them.
  • It is not a basis for a legal finding. CDFW attaches no warranty of positional accuracy to the tracker and states that the presence or absence of data does not support any finding under local, state, or federal law. The tool also does not show land ownership — wolves frequently use private land, and access restrictions to it should be respected.

This data is collected to help mitigate livestock conflict, not for ecological research. The assistant is instructed to report it at the resolution it was published, and to decline to infer behaviour, causes, or predictions from it.

How it works

This app is powered by GLEN, DSE's open-source framework for AI-assisted geospatial exploration. Open datasets stream straight to your browser from cloud-native formats — no tile server in between — and an AI assistant answers questions by writing live queries against the same data. Full technical details are in the GLEN documentation.

🌱 Low-carbon, open models. Be patient. While much faster than most humans, GLEN uses more environmentally-friendly AI models. (Check out the carbon dashboard 🌱 to see the CO₂/energy use of this deployment.)

Exploring the map

The map is built on MapLibre GL — the open-source engine behind familiar Mapbox-style interactive maps. Scroll or pinch to zoom, drag to pan, and hold Ctrl and drag to tilt and rotate the view, useful for reading terrain in 3D across the mountainous wolf range.

The Overlays panel lists every data layer, organized into groups. Toggle a layer on or off with its checkbox, and hover any feature for a tooltip of its live attributes — names, categories, counts — straight from the source data. When layers overlap, use the send-to-back button (↩) in the Overlays header to drop the topmost layer beneath the others.

Base maps

All data layers are drawn over a base map. Use the base-map switcher on the map to choose among three backdrops; NatGeo is the default. NatGeo and Satellite are terrain-enabled, so Ctrl-drag tilts them into 3D.

Asking the assistant

The Wolf Data Assistant understands plain English and does two kinds of things: it can drive the map for you, and it can answer analytical questions about the data. Just type what you want — if it can't do something, it will say so and usually suggest the closest thing it can.

Map operations happen right away on the layers already loaded:

  • Fly somewhere: "Zoom to the Whaleback pack territory", "Fly to Lassen County."
  • Show or hide a layer: "Turn on present-day connectivity", "Show the CWHR habitat types."
  • Filter or restyle: "Show only GAP status 1 and 2 protected lands", "Make farmland 50% transparent."

Analytical questions are answered by writing live spatial queries over the underlying data:

  • "Which protected lands overlap with wolf pack territories?"
  • "What habitat types dominate inside the Whaleback territory?"
  • "Which state assembly districts contain wolf activity?"
📐 Data and methods, not opinions. The assistant is deliberately scoped to computing what you asked for and telling you how it computed it — which dataset, how a feature was defined, at what resolution, over what time window. It is instructed not to interpret results, not to infer behaviour or causes from wolf positions, not to predict where wolves will go, and not to fill gaps from its own background knowledge. If you ask what a number means, it will say that judgment is yours and give you the numbers that bear on it. You are the subject-matter expert; it is the query writer.
✏️ Draw an area to ask about it. Use the draw tool to sketch a polygon — a proposed corridor, a county, a watershed — then ask the assistant about it: "What share of the area I drew is conserved land?", "How much grazing land falls inside this polygon?" Your drawn shape becomes a live spatial filter for any query.

Pack Territories, Latest Cells & Cell-to-Cell Movement (CDFW)
California Gray Wolf Monitoring from the CDFW Wolf Program — three map layers from one collection: approximate pack territory polygons (Feb 2025, to within roughly 5–10 miles); the cells collars were most recently reported in; and 30 days of cell-to-cell movement for collared wolves and dispersers. The six territory polygons are the packs CDFW recognized in February 2025 (Whaleback, Harvey, Lassen, Ice Cave, Diamond/Beyem Seyo, Yowlumni); CDFW has recognized more since, and cells are labelled by the wolf's origin pack rather than its current one. Positions are ~37 km² (14 sq mi) hexagonal cells, not GPS points, and no path between consecutive cells was observed — see reading the wolf movement data.
